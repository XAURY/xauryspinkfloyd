About the album

Meddle is Pink Floyd's sixth studio album, recorded in between tours across Britain, Europe, the Far East and America. It is seen as the bridge between the Syd Barrett-influenced band of the 1960s and the Roger Waters-led band of the later 1970s. Unlike the albums that followed, it was a true group effort, with Waters mainly writing lyrics and playing bass.

The band started the year with no songs and no clear direction, so they invented a series of experiments, which eventually grew into the 23-minute "Echoes" that fills side two. The album is more focused than Atom Heart Mother, and critics welcomed it. Rolling Stone said it confirmed Gilmour as a real shaping force in the band, though Melody Maker thought it sounded like the soundtrack to a film that didn't exist.

The title is a pun on "medal" and "meddle", as in to interfere. The cover, by Hipgnosis, shows an ear underwater collecting ripples of sound. Storm Thorgerson had first suggested a close-up of a baboon's backside, but the band, phoning from Japan while on tour, preferred the ear. Thorgerson later called it his least favourite Pink Floyd sleeve. The gatefold has a group photo that was the last one of the band until 1987.

It reached number three in the UK but only number 70 in the US, where the band's record label did little to promote it. It later sold well there and was certified double platinum.

Song stories

The album came out of a long search for ideas, and several of its best-known moments began as studio accidents.

How the sessions went

The band began at EMI's Abbey Road studio, but it only had eight-track recording, which they found too limiting. They moved their best material to 16-track tape at the smaller AIR and Morgan studios and carried on there. With no songs ready, they tried exercises like each member playing on a separate track with no idea what the others were doing, with a random tempo, around an agreed chord structure. The results were named "Nothings", then "Son of Nothings", and the working title of the whole project became "Return of the Son of Nothing".

The engineer John Leckie remembered sessions that began in the afternoon and ended early the next morning, with little to show for it. Their label manager would drop in with some wine. The band also spent days at AIR trying to make music from household objects, an idea they came back to between The Dark Side of the Moon and Wish You Were Here.

One of These Days

Built around a pulsing bass line that Gilmour created by playing through a Binson Echorec delay. Waters and Gilmour played it together on two basses, one with old strings. The one spoken line, about cutting someone into little pieces, is Nick Mason, recorded at double speed in a high voice and played back at normal speed. The band first played it live for a BBC Radio 1 session on 30 September 1971, and it was released as a single in the US on 29 November, backed with "Fearless".

A Pillow of Winds

A rare quiet, acoustic love song for the band, which flows out of "One of These Days" over the sound of wind. Its title comes from games of mahjong that Waters, Mason and their wives played in the south of France.

Fearless

It ends with a recording of Liverpool football fans on the Kop at Anfield singing "You'll Never Walk Alone", fading out in heavy reverb.

San Tropez

A relaxed, jazz-flavoured pop song with a shuffle beat, written by Waters after the band's trip to the south of France in 1970.

Seamus

A joke blues featuring the howling of Seamus, the dog of the singer Steve Marriott, which Gilmour was looking after at the time. It often comes top of lists of the band's worst songs. For the Live at Pompeii film it was remade as "Mademoiselle Nobs", with a different dog and no words.

Echoes

First played live as "Return of the Son of Nothing" in Norwich on 22 April 1971, and then worked on for around three months in three studios. It started as a studio experiment: Richard Wright ran a single piano note through a Leslie speaker and got a sonar-like ping. The band could never recreate it, so the original demo sound stayed on the record. It opens the piece.

Gilmour's guitar added to the experiments. One of the sounds came from plugging his guitar into a wah-wah pedal backwards (the guitar into the pedal's output, and the pedal's input into the amp), a trick the band had used live since 1970 in "Embryo". Unlike with Atom Heart Mother, the 16-track tape meant they could build the piece in stages instead of playing it in one take. Some leftover material from the sessions later became "Brain Damage" on The Dark Side of the Moon.

On tour

The band toured the US and Canada from October to November 1971, their biggest tour up to then. It was the last time they played "Embryo", "Fat Old Sun" and "Cymbaline" live.
