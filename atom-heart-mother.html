About the album

Atom Heart Mother is Pink Floyd's fifth studio album and their first number one in the UK. It reached number 55 in the US and later went gold there. The band started work on it after finishing their part of the soundtrack for the film Zabriskie Point in Rome, an experience that ended on a sour note, and then returned to London in early 1970 to rehearse. They used leftovers from the Rome sessions to build new material.

The idea is similar to Ummagumma: the whole band plays together on the first half, and the second half is given to the individual members. Side one is the 23-minute title suite, written with the composer Ron Geesin, and side two has one song each from Waters, Wright and Gilmour, followed by a sound-effects piece conceived mainly by Nick Mason.

The cover, by Hipgnosis, shows a Holstein cow in a field, with no text and no photo of the band. It was the group's answer to their space-rock image: they asked for "something plain". The designer Storm Thorgerson drove into the countryside near Potters Bar and photographed the first cow he saw. Its owner said she was called Lulubelle III.

Reviews were mixed, and the band themselves have been critical. Gilmour has called it a low point and Waters has made clear he would never want to play it again. Others see it as the high point of their experimental period.

Song stories

The title suite had a troubled birth, and the second side shows three songwriters finding their own voices.

Atom Heart Mother: the first ideas

The suite grew out of instrumental ideas from the early 1970 rehearsals, including the chord progression of the main theme, which Gilmour had been calling "Theme from an Imaginary Western". The first known live performance was at Hull University on 17 January 1970, and the band felt that playing it live helped shape it into something manageable.

Atom Heart Mother: the recording

It was the first recording made on the studio's new eight-track tape machine and mixing desk. According to Nick Mason, EMI would not let the band cut and splice the tape, so he and Waters had to play drums and bass through the entire 23 minutes in one go, with the other instruments added afterwards. Geesin disagreed, saying the tapes he was given were a patchwork of short sections.

By March the band had a backing track but felt it lacked focus. They had met Geesin through the Rolling Stones' tour manager, Sam Cutler, and liked his tape-editing skills. They gave him the tapes and asked him to write an orchestral arrangement while they toured the US. Geesin has said the work was hard because nobody was sure what they wanted and the band couldn't read music. When the orchestra's session players, who were unimpressed by his taste for avant-garde music, made life difficult at the June session, the choir conductor John Alldis stepped in to conduct instead.

Atom Heart Mother: the name

It was first known as "The Amazing Pudding", and Geesin's score called it "Untitled Epic". When the band needed a title for a BBC Radio 1 broadcast on 16 July 1970, Geesin pointed Waters to that day's Evening Standard. A headline there, "Atom Heart Mother Named", was about a woman fitted with a plutonium-powered pacemaker. The first section, "Father's Shout", is named after the jazz pianist Earl "Fatha" Hines, and other parts were named after the cover art.

Atom Heart Mother: on stage

An improved version with Geesin's written parts was played at the Bath Festival on 27 June 1970, with the John Alldis Choir and the Philip Jones Brass Ensemble, and again in Hyde Park on 18 July. The band later took a full brass section and choir on tour just for the suite, which lost money, and the hired musicians changed from show to show. At one concert in Aachen they discovered they had left the sheet music behind. A shorter version without brass or choir stayed in the set until the last performance on 22 May 1972 in Amsterdam. When the first live performance of what became The Dark Side of the Moon broke down in Brighton, the band played this instead.

If

Waters's gentle folk ballad, with Waters on acoustic guitar. Pink Floyd rarely played it live, but Waters often performed it at solo shows more than a decade later.

Summer '68

Wright's song, with brass. It was reportedly about Wright and a groupie on tour, and its working title was "One Night Stand". It came out as a Japanese single in 1971 and is the only track on the album the band never played live.

Fat Old Sun

According to Mason, Gilmour had written little by then and was told to stay at EMI until he came up with a song for the album. The result was this folk-flavoured tune, which he still counts as a favourite. It was a regular in the band's 1970–71 shows and became a staple of Gilmour's solo concerts in 2006.

Alan's Psychedelic Breakfast

It began when Waters experimented with the rhythm of a dripping tap. Mason recorded the sound of the band's roadie Alan Styles making and eating breakfast in his own kitchen, and the band added music recorded at EMI. At a show in Sheffield on 22 December 1970, the band paused between sections to eat and drink their own breakfast on stage. On the original LP, the dripping tap carries on into the final groove, so it plays on indefinitely.

Visitors and refusals

Syd Barrett was making his own album, Barrett, around the same time, with help from Gilmour and Wright, and he sometimes dropped in on the band's sessions. The director Stanley Kubrick wanted the title track for A Clockwork Orange but the band refused, because he couldn't say which parts he wanted or what he'd do with them. The album does appear in a record-shop scene in the film.
